Why food safety matters

about 2 min read5-question check

In this lesson

  • Explain what foodborne illness is and how food can make people sick.
  • Name the four groups at higher risk: young children, older adults, pregnant people, and people with weak immune systems.
  • Recognize TCS foods and keep them cold at 41°F or below or hot at 135°F or above.
  • List the 5 CDC risk factors and the 3 types of hazards (biological, chemical, physical).
  • Describe what a food handler does every shift to keep food safe.

The lesson

Halftime. The line is long, and a tray of hot dogs has been sitting on the counter for a while. Is it still safe? Hi, I'm Inspector Dex. In this lesson, you'll learn why food safety matters, and why it starts with you.

Foodborne illness means getting sick from food or drink. Germs or chemicals in food can cause it. People may get stomach cramps, vomiting, diarrhea, or fever. And objects in food, like glass, can hurt people too. The CDC estimates that about 48 million people in the United States get sick from food every year.

Some people get much sicker. Young children. Older adults. Pregnant people. And people with weak immune systems, like someone getting cancer treatment. Many of them are in your line at every event. You can't tell by looking who is at risk, so every plate must be safe.

Germs grow easily in some foods. We call them TCS foods: Time and Temperature Control for Safety. Meat, chicken, hot dogs, eggs, milk, cooked rice and beans, cut melon, sliced tomatoes, and cut lettuce are all TCS foods. Keep them cold at 41 degrees Fahrenheit or below, or hot at 135 degrees or above.

The CDC names five main risk factors behind foodborne illness outbreaks. One: food held at the wrong temperature. Two: food not cooked enough, like chicken under 165 degrees. Three: dirty or contaminated equipment. Four: food from unsafe sources, like food made at someone's home. Five: poor personal hygiene, like not washing your hands.

Anything that can make food unsafe is a hazard. There are three types. Biological: germs like bacteria and viruses. Chemical: cleaner or sanitizer that gets into food. Physical: objects like glass, metal, hair, or a bandage.

So what is your job? Wash your hands the right way, and often. Check temperatures and write them down. Keep surfaces clean and sanitized. And if you have vomiting or diarrhea, tell your manager before your shift, or right away if it starts at work. With vomiting or diarrhea, you must not work.

Back to our hot dogs. The thermometer reads 120 degrees. That is below 135, so it is too cold. Tell your manager right away. If nobody knows how long it sat out, throw it out. And remember: your local rules or your venue may be stricter.

The one thing to remember: you are the last check before food reaches a customer. Clean hands, the right temperatures, and speaking up keep people safe. See you in the next lesson.

Remember

  • Foodborne illness means getting sick from food or drink. The CDC estimates about 48 million people in the U.S. get sick from food each year.
  • Higher risk: young children, older adults, pregnant people, and people with weak immune systems. You can't tell by looking, so every plate must be safe.
  • TCS foods (meat, chicken, eggs, milk, cooked rice and beans, cut melon, cut tomatoes, cut leafy greens) need time and temperature control: cold at 41°F or below, hot at 135°F or above (Food Code 3-501.16).
  • The 5 CDC risk factors: improper holding temperatures, inadequate cooking, contaminated equipment, food from unsafe sources, poor personal hygiene.
  • The 3 hazard types: biological (germs), chemical (cleaners, sanitizers), physical (glass, metal, hair, bandages).
  • Your job: wash your hands, check and log temperatures, clean and sanitize, and tell your manager before your shift, or right away at work, if you have vomiting or diarrhea; you must not work while you have them. Your local rules or your venue may be stricter.

Check yourself

Pick an answer — you will see right away if it is right, and why. Get 4 of 5 right to complete the lesson.

1. Which of these is a TCS food?

Cut tomatoes are a TCS food in the FDA Food Code (1-201.10), so they must be kept cold at 41°F or below.

2. What are the safe holding temperatures for TCS food?

The FDA Food Code (3-501.16) says cold TCS food stays at 41°F or below and hot TCS food at 135°F or above; your local rules or your venue may be stricter.

3. Which group has a higher risk of getting very sick from food?

The CDC lists pregnant people, young children, older adults, and people with weak immune systems as higher risk for food poisoning.

4. A cook brings tamales made at home to sell at the stand. Which CDC risk factor is this?

Food must come from approved sources, and food made in a private home may not be served in a food establishment (Food Code 3-201.11).

5. A piece of glass falls into a pan of salsa. What type of hazard is it?

Objects like glass, metal, hair, or a bandage are physical hazards; the Food Code defines a hazard as biological, chemical, or physical (1-201.10).

Where this comes from
  • CDC estimates that each year about 48 million people in the United States (roughly 1 in 6) get sick from foodborne diseases, 128,000 are hospitalized, and 3,000 die.CDC, Estimates of Foodborne Illness in the United States (2011 estimates; Scallan et al., Emerging Infectious Diseases, 2011), cdc.gov/foodborneburden
  • Adults 65 and older, children younger than 5, people with weakened immune systems, and pregnant people are more likely to get food poisoning and to get seriously ill.CDC, 'People with a Higher Risk of Food Poisoning' (cdc.gov/food-safety); see also 2022 FDA Food Code 1-201.10(B), definition of 'Highly susceptible population' (immunocompromised people, preschool-age children, older adults)
  • TCS (time/temperature control for safety) food needs time/temperature control to limit germ growth or toxin formation; it includes raw or cooked animal foods, heat-treated plant foods such as cooked rice and beans, raw seed sprouts, cut melons, cut leafy greens, cut tomatoes, and garlic-in-oil mixtures.2022 FDA Food Code 1-201.10(B), definition of 'Time/temperature control for safety food'
  • TCS food must be held hot at 57°C (135°F) or above, or cold at 5°C (41°F) or less.2022 FDA Food Code 3-501.16(A)
  • Poultry must be cooked to 74°C (165°F) or above for less than 1 second (instantaneous).2022 FDA Food Code 3-401.11(A)(3)
  • The five major foodborne illness risk factors identified by CDC are: food from unsafe sources, inadequate cooking, improper holding temperatures, contaminated equipment, and poor personal hygiene.2022 FDA Food Code, Preface and Annex 4 (Management of Food Safety Practices - Achieving Active Managerial Control of Foodborne Illness Risk Factors)
  • Food must be obtained from sources that comply with law; food prepared in a private home may not be used or offered for human consumption in a food establishment.2022 FDA Food Code 3-201.11(A) and (B)
  • A hazard is a biological, chemical, or physical property that may cause an unacceptable consumer health risk.2022 FDA Food Code 1-201.10(B), definition of 'Hazard'
  • Poisonous or toxic materials such as cleaners and sanitizers must be stored so they cannot contaminate food, equipment, or single-service items.2022 FDA Food Code 7-201.11
  • Food employees must report symptoms such as vomiting and diarrhea to the person in charge, and a food employee with vomiting or diarrhea is excluded, which means the person is prevented from working as a food employee in the food establishment or entering it as an employee.2022 FDA Food Code 2-201.11(A), 2-201.12(A)(1), and 1-201.10(B) definition of 'Exclude'
  • Food employees must clean their hands using the required procedure and at the required times (for example after touching raw food, after using the toilet, and when changing tasks).2022 FDA Food Code 2-301.12 and 2-301.14
  • Equipment food-contact surfaces and utensils must be cleaned and sanitized; when used with TCS food they must be cleaned at least every 4 hours if in constant use.2022 FDA Food Code 4-602.11
  • TCS food held without temperature control must be marked with a time; food in unmarked containers or past the time limit must be discarded, which supports 'if nobody knows how long it sat out, throw it out'.2022 FDA Food Code 3-501.19(B)
  • The Food Code is a model that state and local agencies adopt, sometimes with changes, so local rules or a venue's own rules may be stricter.2022 FDA Food Code, Preface (purpose of the model Food Code and its adoption by states and localities)
